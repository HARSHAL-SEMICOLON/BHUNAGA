# Managing pests without wasting money

Two things cost Indian farmers more than pests themselves: spraying when there
is nothing to spray for, and spraying in conditions where the chemical never
reaches the pest.

## Spray only when it is worth it

Every pest has an **economic threshold** — the population at which the damage it
will cause is worth more than the cost of controlling it. Below that threshold,
spraying loses money even when it works perfectly.

| Crop | Pest | Spray when |
|---|---|---|
| Cotton | Bollworm | 10 percent of bolls damaged, or 8 moths per trap per night for 3 nights |
| Tomato | Fruit borer | 5 percent of fruits damaged |
| Soybean | Defoliators | 25 percent leaf area eaten before flowering |
| Tur | Pod borer | 1 larva per plant at flowering |
| Onion | Thrips | 30 thrips per plant |
| Grapes | Downy mildew | first spots seen, plus humid weather forecast |

Note how high some of these are. A crop can lose a quarter of its leaf area
before flowering and still yield normally — plants compensate. Spraying at the
first chewed leaf is money thrown away.

Walk the field twice a week and count on 10 plants chosen at random from
different corners. Counting on the worst-looking plants gives the wrong answer.

## When to spray

Conditions decide whether the chemical works at all:

- **Wind under 10 km/h.** Above 15 km/h the spray drifts off the field entirely.
  In dead calm below 3 km/h droplets hang and drift unpredictably later.
- **No rain for at least 4 to 6 hours afterwards.** Rain before the chemical dries
  washes it straight off and the whole operation is repeated.
- **Below 35 degrees.** In greater heat droplets evaporate before landing.
- **Early morning or late evening.** Also when pollinators are least active.

Spraying at the wrong time is not a partial success. It is usually a complete
loss of the chemical, the labour, the water and the fuel.

## Rotate the chemical group

Pests develop resistance when the same chemical group is used repeatedly.
Bollworm resistance to synthetic pyrethroids in Indian cotton is the textbook
case — it made an entire cheap chemical family useless.

Rule: **never use the same group twice in a row**, and no more than twice in one
season. The group is printed on the label. Two products with different brand
names are frequently the same molecule, which is how farmers rotate without
realising they have not.

## Cheap methods that work

- **Pheromone traps** — 5 per acre. They tell you when the moths arrive, so you
  spray at the right moment rather than on a calendar.
- **Yellow sticky traps** — 10 per acre for whitefly and aphid. Yellow attracts
  them; they stick and cannot breed.
- **Bird perches** — 10 per acre, just a forked stick. Birds eat a large share of
  the caterpillars. Costs nothing.
- **Trap crops** — marigold around a tomato field draws fruit borer away from the
  crop. Castor around cotton does the same for Spodoptera.
- **Neem** — 5 percent neem seed kernel extract works on young larvae and sucking
  pests. It does not kill on contact like a synthetic; it stops feeding and
  moulting, so judge it after 3 or 4 days, not the same evening.
- **Removing affected plants by hand** early is often enough on a small plot, and
  is the only real control for virus diseases.

## Protect yourself

Pesticide poisoning is common and under-reported.

- Read the label. The colour triangle shows toxicity: red is extremely toxic,
  yellow highly toxic, blue moderately, green slightly.
- Wear full sleeves, gloves, and a mask covering nose and mouth.
- Never spray against the wind.
- Never eat, drink or smoke while spraying.
- Wash thoroughly and change clothes immediately afterwards.
- Never reuse an empty pesticide container for water or food. Puncture it and
  bury it.
- Keep chemicals locked away from children and animals.

**Observe the pre-harvest interval** printed on the label — the number of days
that must pass between the last spray and harvest. Ignoring it leaves residue on
produce that gets rejected at market and is unsafe to eat.

## What to do if someone is poisoned

Move them to fresh air, remove contaminated clothing, wash the skin with soap
and water, and get to a hospital immediately. **Take the pesticide container or
its label with you** — the doctor needs to know the molecule to treat correctly.
Do not induce vomiting unless the label says to.
