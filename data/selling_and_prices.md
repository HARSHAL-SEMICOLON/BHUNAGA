# Selling produce and reading market prices

## How mandi prices are quoted

Prices at an APMC mandi are reported as three figures, almost always **per
quintal** (100 kg):

- **Minimum** — the lowest grade sold that day
- **Maximum** — the best grade sold that day
- **Modal** — the price at which the largest quantity changed hands

**The modal price is the one to plan with.** The maximum is usually a small lot
of exceptional quality and is not what an average consignment will fetch.
Farmers who plan around the maximum are routinely disappointed, and it is worth
knowing that before loading a truck.

Where prices are published: the Agmarknet portal (agmarknet.gov.in), the eNAM
platform, the mandi's own notice board, and this app's Market screen, which
reads the same government feed.

## Minimum Support Price

The central government announces an MSP before each season for 22 crops,
including wheat, paddy, tur, gram, soybean, cotton and sugarcane (as FRP).

What MSP does and does not mean:

- It is a **floor at government procurement centres**, not a guaranteed market
  price. If no centre is procuring your crop in your area, the MSP does not
  reach you.
- Procurement is much more active for wheat, paddy and some pulses than for
  vegetables, which have no MSP at all.
- To sell at a procurement centre you generally need registration on the state
  portal, the 7/12 extract, an Aadhaar card and a bank account in your own name.
  **Register before the season opens** — registration windows close, and a farmer
  arriving with a loaded cart and no registration cannot sell.

## When to sell

Prices for storable crops follow a fairly reliable pattern: they are lowest at
peak arrival, right when everybody harvests, and recover 2 to 4 months later.

For onion, potato, tur and gram, holding part of the crop is often worth more
than the storage cost. For tomato and leafy vegetables it is not — they will not
keep.

Against holding, weigh honestly:

- Storage losses (5 to 30 percent depending on crop and store)
- Interest, if the crop is financed
- The immediate need for cash — a distress sale to repay a loan on its due date
  is the single most common way farmers lose money on an otherwise good crop
- The risk that the price falls further

A practical middle path is to sell in three or four lots across the season
instead of one. Nobody predicts the peak; splitting removes the need to.

## Where to sell

**APMC mandi** — the traditional route. Sale is by open auction through a
licensed commission agent. Deductions for commission, weighing, loading and
market fee typically come to 6 to 10 percent, and should be itemised on the
patti. Ask for it in writing.

**eNAM** — an online all-India trading platform linking mandis. Buyers from
other states can bid, which widens the market. Requires registration and lot
assaying.

**Farmer Producer Organisation** — selling collectively as an FPO gives real
bargaining power. A truckload negotiates a better price than ten cartloads, and
an FPO can sell directly to processors and retail chains, bypassing several
intermediaries.

**Direct to consumer** — weekly farmers markets and residential society sales
give the highest price per kg but consume time and need small, steady volumes.
Best for vegetables and fruit.

**Contract farming** — a fixed price agreed before sowing. It removes price risk,
which is genuinely valuable, but read the quality clauses very carefully. The
common failure is the buyer rejecting a consignment on quality grounds after the
farmer has already grown the crop to their specification.

## Practical points that change the price you get

- **Grade before you go.** Sorted, uniform produce fetches more than a mixed lot,
  and grading costs only labour you already have.
- **Clean packing.** Damaged and dirty produce drags the whole lot's grade down.
- **Arrive early.** Prices often soften as the day's arrivals build up.
- **Check the day's arrivals first.** If everyone is bringing onion today,
  tomorrow may be better. Arrival volumes are published alongside prices.
- **Keep your own record** of what you sold, when, at what price and what was
  deducted. Over two seasons this tells you more about when to sell your crop in
  your mandi than any general advice can.

## Watch out for

- Verbal-only sales with no receipt
- Deductions not shown on the patti
- Payment promised "next week" with nothing in writing — insist on the payment
  terms before the auction, not after
- Anyone charging a fee to arrange a government scheme or MSP registration.
  Those are free.
