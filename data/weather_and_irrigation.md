# Weather, water and protecting the crop

## Reading a forecast as a farmer

A forecast is not a promise. Treat rain probability as a planning tool:

- **Below 30 percent** — carry on as planned
- **30 to 60 percent** — do not spray, do not top-dress fertiliser
- **Above 60 percent** — assume it will rain; clear the drains, postpone harvest
  of anything that must stay dry

The number that matters for spraying is not whether it rains today, but whether
it rains in the **4 to 6 hours after you spray**. Rain before the chemical dries
washes it off completely.

## Heat

Above 38 degrees most crops shut down photosynthesis and, at flowering, drop
flowers.

- Irrigate in the early morning or late evening, never in the middle of the day
- Mulch with crop residue to keep the soil cool and hold moisture
- A shade net over nursery beds and high-value vegetables is worth its cost
- Foliar potassium sprays improve heat tolerance somewhat
- Do not transplant seedlings during a heat spell; wait for a cooler few days

## Unseasonal rain and hail

Maharashtra now gets damaging unseasonal rain most years, particularly in
February and March, when rabi crops are at harvest.

Before it arrives:

- Harvest anything that is ready. A crop in the shed is safe; a crop in the field
  is not
- Clear field drains — waterlogging for even 2 days kills tur, gram and onion
- Support tall crops such as sugarcane and banana
- Cover harvested produce and move it off the threshing floor

After it passes:

- Drain standing water as fast as possible
- Expect fungal disease within a few days and plan for it
- A light foliar nutrient spray helps the crop recover
- **Report the damage within 72 hours** if you have crop insurance. This deadline
  is the commonest reason genuine claims are rejected

## Frost

A risk in December and January in parts of north Maharashtra, mainly for grapes,
mango, vegetables and gram.

Light irrigation on the evening a frost is forecast raises the soil temperature
overnight and is the cheapest effective protection. Smoke from burning damp
straw on the windward side also helps. Wind breaks reduce frost settling in low
spots.

## Drought and water shortage

When water is short, the question is not how to irrigate everything a little,
but which stage to protect. Each crop has a critical stage where water stress
costs the most yield:

| Crop | Protect water at |
|---|---|
| Wheat | Crown root initiation, 20 to 25 days |
| Onion | Bulb formation |
| Cotton | Flowering and boll development |
| Soybean | Pod filling |
| Tomato | Flowering and fruit set |
| Gram | Pod formation |
| Sugarcane | Grand growth period |

One well-timed irrigation at the critical stage yields more than three spread
evenly through the season.

Other measures: mulching, wider spacing when sowing into a poor forecast,
choosing a shorter-duration variety, and switching part of the area to a less
thirsty crop.

## Drip and sprinkler

Drip irrigation saves 40 to 60 percent of water and typically raises yield 20 to
30 percent, because the water goes where the roots are and the leaves stay dry.
Dry leaves alone cut fungal disease substantially.

It also lets you fertigate — apply fertiliser dissolved in the irrigation water,
in small frequent doses, which the plant uses far more efficiently than a single
broadcast application.

Subsidy is available under PMKSY Per Drop More Crop and, in the project
districts, under PoCRA. Rates are generally 55 percent for smallholders and
marginal farmers and 45 percent for others.

Maintenance is the part people skip: flush the laterals monthly, clean the filter
weekly, and treat with acid once a season if your water is hard. A clogged drip
system quietly waters one part of the field and not another.

## Rainwater harvesting

A farm pond of 20 x 20 x 3 metres holds about 1,200 cubic metres — roughly enough
for one protective irrigation on 2 acres. On a rain-fed farm that single
irrigation, given at the critical stage, is often what separates a normal harvest
from a failed one.

Recharging your own borewell with roof and field runoff is cheap and directly
raises the water available at the end of the season.
