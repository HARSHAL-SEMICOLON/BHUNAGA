# Making vermicompost at home

Earthworms turn farm waste into the best soil amendment available, at almost no
cash cost. This is the practical method, at the scale a smallholder can actually
manage.

## Why it is worth the trouble

Worm castings hold roughly 5 times the available nitrogen, 7 times the available
phosphorus and 11 times the available potassium of the surrounding soil. More
importantly they carry live microbes and humus, which chemical fertiliser does
not. Soil with good organic matter holds water longer, drains better after rain,
and lets roots go deeper.

A field with low organic carbon does not respond well to fertiliser — the
nutrients wash straight through. Raising organic matter makes everything else
you spend money on work better.

## Which worm

Use **Eisenia fetida** (red wiggler) or **Eudrilus eugeniae** (African
nightcrawler). These are surface feeders that live in decomposing matter.

Do not use the deep-burrowing earthworms found in your field — they eat soil,
not waste, and will not work in a bed.

Start with about 1 kg of worms (roughly 1,000 worms) per bed of 1 x 1 x 0.6
metres. They double in population every 60 to 70 days in good conditions, so one
purchase is enough forever.

## Building the bed

A simple shaded pit or a heap on the ground both work.

1. **Site** — full shade, protected from direct sun and rain. Under a tree or in
   a thatched shed. Direct sun kills the worms within hours.
2. **Size** — 3 metres long, 1 metre wide, 0.6 metres high is a manageable unit.
   Wider than 1 metre makes the middle hard to reach.
3. **Base layer** — 5 cm of broken bricks or coarse sand for drainage, then 15 cm
   of loamy soil.
4. **Bedding** — 5 cm of cow dung, at least 15 days old. Fresh dung generates
   heat and will kill the worms.
5. **Waste layer** — chopped crop residue, dry leaves, vegetable waste, in layers
   with more partly decomposed dung.
6. **Introduce the worms**, then cover with a gunny sack or straw.

## Feeding it

Good inputs: crop residue, dry leaves, vegetable and fruit peel, weeds that have
not seeded, cattle dung, banana pseudostem.

Keep out: meat, oil, milk products, salt, citrus peel in large amounts, and
anything sprayed with pesticide recently. Do not put in plastic, glass or metal —
obvious, but it is the commonest reason a batch is ruined.

Chop material to 5 cm pieces. Whole stalks take three times as long.

## Looking after it

- **Moisture** — like a wrung-out sponge, about 60 to 70 percent. Squeeze a
  handful: it should hold together and release one or two drops, not a stream.
  Sprinkle water daily in summer, every 2 or 3 days in winter.
- **Temperature** — 20 to 30 degrees. Above 35 degrees the worms stop working and
  start dying. Extra shade and more frequent watering in May and June.
- **Do not turn it** like ordinary compost. Turning disturbs the worms and slows
  everything down.
- **Bad smell means something is wrong** — usually too wet or too much fresh
  green material. Add dry leaves and stop watering for a few days.

## Harvesting

Ready in 45 to 60 days in summer, 60 to 90 days in winter. It is done when the
material is dark brown or black, crumbly, loose, and smells of earth rather than
dung.

To separate worms from castings: stop watering for 2 or 3 days, then heap the
material into a cone in sunlight. The worms move down and inward away from the
light. Scrape the outside off every 20 minutes until only a ball of worms
remains at the bottom, ready for the next bed.

Sieve the castings through a 3 mm mesh. What passes through is finished
vermicompost. What stays behind goes back into the next batch.

## Yield and use

One bed of 3 x 1 x 0.6 metres produces about 400 to 500 kg per cycle, giving
roughly 2 to 2.5 tonnes a year from one bed.

Application rates:

- Field crops: 2 tonnes per acre before sowing
- Vegetables: 3 to 4 tonnes per acre
- Fruit trees: 5 to 10 kg per tree per year
- Nursery mix: 1 part vermicompost to 3 parts soil

## Vermiwash

The liquid that drains from the bed is a good foliar feed. Collect it, dilute 1
part to 10 parts water, and spray on the crop in the early morning or evening.
Do not spray it in the middle of a hot day.

## Cost

Setting up one bed costs 3,000 to 5,000 rupees including the worms and a shade
structure. It pays for itself in one year against the cost of buying an
equivalent quantity of compost, and the worm stock never needs buying again.
